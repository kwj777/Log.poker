---
title: She told me she had queens
description: She made it $12 with pocket queens, under-bet a flop that had already beaten her, and folded the turn. The only evidence she had queens at all is that she said so.
---

I know exactly one thing about her hand, and she is the only reason I know it.

She made it twelve dollars before the flop. I called from later position with the eight and nine of one suit, which is a hand I have been more careful with since [the last time I wrote about one](/blog/the-2-dollar-limp-that-cost-400): there was a raise in front of me and I called it, in position, which is the version of that hand that actually works.

The flop came with two eights in it.

::: figure told-me-queens
Both of mine were the same suit, though I could not tell you which. Her two are face down because nobody ever turned them over.
:::

Trip eights, on the flop, against the raiser. She bet, and she bet small.

I called. The nine came on the turn, which gave me eights full of nines and gave her nothing at all, and she folded before a river was dealt.

Then, because the hand was over and there was nothing left to protect, she told me she had pocket queens.

## What she was betting into

::: stat Two outs
Both of them queens. On the flop, when she bet, I was 90.4% to win the hand; on the turn, when she folded, 95.4%. Every runout enumerated rather than sampled, and in all of them her only winning card is a queen.
:::

That is the whole shape of it. From the moment the flop landed she needed one of exactly two cards left in the deck, and she was the one doing the betting.

For what it is worth, the flop was not a likely one. A suited connector flops trips or better about [once in sixty-nine](/pot-odds) tries. She was not unlucky to run into it so much as unlucky that it was the one time in sixty-nine.

## The under-bet, fairly

I want to be careful here, because betting small with queens on a paired board is not a stupid play. It is a common one, and it comes from an instinct that is basically sound.

Queens are a big hand right up until the board pairs, and then they are a big hand with a problem. She wanted to keep betting without committing, to stay in charge of a pot she had opened, and to find out where she stood without paying much to find out. Every one of those wants is reasonable.

The trouble is that a small bet on a paired board does not deliver any of them:

- **It does not fold out an eight.** Nothing holding an eight is folding, at any price, ever. The one hand she needed gone is the one hand that is never going anywhere.
- **It does not charge anything enough.** A draw gets a price it would take all night, and so does a hand like mine, which is not drawing at all.
- **It does not answer her question.** A bet that every hand calls tells you nothing when it gets called. She paid to buy information and the information was blank.

So the bet built a pot she could not win, told her nothing, and then on the turn she folded to the pressure she had funded. That is not a disaster of a hand. It is just a sequence where every individual decision was defensible and the sizing quietly made all of them worse.

## The part that is actually about me

Here is the bit worth sitting with, and it is not about her play at all.

**I never saw her cards.**

She folded on the turn. The queens, if they were queens, went into the muck face down. There is no version of this hand in which anybody verified anything. What I have is a sentence she said afterwards, in a good mood, having just made what she plainly considered a disciplined laydown.

I am not accusing her of anything. I have no reason at all to think she was lying. But look at how many ways that sentence gets said without anybody intending to mislead:

- She had queens, and said so.
- She had jacks, and rounded up, the way everybody rounds up.
- She folded something she was embarrassed by and named a better hand, which is such an ordinary reflex that people do it without noticing.
- She genuinely misremembered, thirty seconds later, which is a thing human beings do constantly and are certain they never do.

All four produce exactly the same sentence. I cannot tell them apart, and neither can you, and no amount of thinking about her face while she said it is going to change that.

And yet. If I do not write it down carefully, *she had queens* is what survives in my head. Six weeks from now it has stopped being a claim and turned into a fact about how she plays, and I am making real decisions with real money against a read whose entire evidence base is that somebody said a thing once.

That is the quiet way a read goes wrong. Not by being wrong at the time. By being unlabeled, and then aging into certainty.

## The rule I use now

When I log a hand, anything somebody told me goes in the note with the word *said* still attached.

**"She said queens."** Never "she had queens."

Four characters, and it is the entire difference: one of them is something I watched happen and the other is testimony from an interested party. Both are worth keeping. They are not worth keeping in the same box, and memory does not have two boxes.

It costs nothing to write the hand down the way it actually reached me. It costs a surprising amount to find out, a year later, that half of what I know about the regulars in my game came from things they told me while stacking their chips.

[Log is free, all of it, and works offline with no account](/app). A hand takes fifteen seconds, and there is a line for the note, which is where the word *said* goes.
