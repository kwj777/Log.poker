There is a moment in a losing session when you reach for your wallet and the thought arrives fully formed: *one more buy-in and I can get it back.*

It sounds like a plan. It is not a plan. It is the loss talking, and it has picked the one moment when I am least able to argue with it.

So I argue with it earlier. Before I sit down, I decide how much I am prepared to lose that day, and when that much is gone, I stand up. That is a stop loss, and this post is about why it works and how to actually honor it, which is the harder half.

## The honest part first

A stop loss does not change the math of any hand. The cards do not know you are down $400. A hand you play well when you are stuck is worth exactly what it would be worth if you were up. If you are playing your best game in a good game, quitting because of the number in front of you costs you money, not saves it.

That is true, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. It is also not the situation a stop loss is for.

## Why it works anyway

A stop loss is not a bet on the cards. It is a bet on me, and specifically on two things I cannot check from inside the session.

**Am I still playing my game?** After a big loss I am not at baseline. I call a bit wider because I want to win a pot. I bluff a spot I would normally give up. None of it feels like tilt. It feels like *finally getting aggressive*. The [tilt guide](/tilt) puts it better than I can: tilt does not feel like tilt at the start, it feels like clarity.

**Is this still a good game?** Sometimes I am losing because the cards are cold. Sometimes I am losing because two players at the table are better than I am, and the cards are only how I am finding out. From the chair those look identical.

Now look at what each choice costs. Take the example player from the [running bad guide](/running-bad): a $1/$2 winner at $10 an hour. Say they are down two buy-ins with three hours left in the session.

If they leave and they were still playing well, they give up three hours of their edge. That is $30, on average.

If they stay and the loss has turned them from a $10 an hour winner into a $10 an hour loser, which is not much of a slide, the three hours cost them $30 instead of earning it. And that is the gentle version. The real cost of playing badly when stuck is the one big pot you should not have been in.

So leaving costs a small, known amount. Staying risks a larger, unknown one, and the person deciding whether it is safe to stay is the one person at the table who cannot judge it.

## Leaving does not lock in the loss

This is the part that makes it hard to stand up. Walking away feels like making the loss permanent, as if staying keeps it open and leaving signs the papers.

It does not work that way. I wrote about this [the last time](/blog/i-fought-the-law): the next session does not know about this one. Getting even tonight and getting even next Tuesday are the same thing in expectation. The only difference is which version of me is playing, and next Tuesday's version has slept.

The money is not lost by standing up. It was lost in the hands. Standing up is the only thing on the table that cannot lose any more of it.

## How I pick the number

I count in buy-ins, not dollars, because it scales with the game and because it matches the physical moment: reaching for more money.

For the example player, losing two full buy-ins over a six hour session happens about one session in 33. Losing three happens about one session in 280. A genuine winner hits those numbers sometimes, so reaching the stop is not proof that I played badly. But it is rare enough that when it happens, something besides ordinary bad luck is worth suspecting, and the decision about what to do next should be made by somebody calm.

Two buy-ins or three is a personal choice, and it should fit your bankroll. If a single day can take a big bite out of your roll, the [bankroll guide](/bankroll) is the better place to start than this post.

What matters more than the exact number is that it exists before the first hand, and that it never moves during the session. A stop loss that gets renegotiated at the table is not a stop loss. It is a starting offer.

## Making the loss complete

Here is the mental part, and it is the one I had to practice.

I stop thinking of it as quitting. The number I chose before I sat down is how long the session is. When it is reached, the session is not cut short. It is *complete*. Same as when I planned to play until midnight and midnight came.

Some things that help me hold to it:

- **Bring only the buy-ins you are willing to lose.** The cleanest stop loss is an empty pocket. If the third buy-in is at home, there is nothing to negotiate.
- **Say the number out loud when you sit down.** To a friend, or just to yourself. A number spoken before the session belongs to a calmer person, and it is easier to keep a promise to them.
- **Decide what happens next before you need to.** Rack up, cash out, walk to the car. Do not stand at the rail watching the next hand. Watching is how you end up back in the seat.
- **Log the session before you drive home.** Writing it down is the line under it. It turns *I am down $400 and I want it back* into *that was a $400 session*, which is a fact, and facts do not ask for anything.

The last one matters most to me. A loss that is still in your head feels open. A loss that is in your log is a row, one of many, and it reads very differently next to all the others.
