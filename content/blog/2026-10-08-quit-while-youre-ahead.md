After I wrote about [setting a stop loss](/blog/the-loss-is-complete), the first question I got was the obvious one. If there is a number where you go home losing, should there be a number where you go home winning?

There is such a thing. It is called a stop win, and plenty of players swear by it: decide before you sit down that once you are up two buy-ins, you rack up and leave. Lock it in. Quit while you are ahead.

I understand the appeal, and I used to do it. I mostly do not anymore, and the reason is the same arithmetic that makes the stop loss work.

## The money does not know you are winning

The cards do not know you are down $400, and they do not know you are up $400 either. A hand you play well is worth the same at the start of the night as it is after a great run. If you are a winning player in a good game, every hour you sit there is worth your hourly rate, and leaving early gives some of it away.

Take the example player from the [running bad guide](/running-bad) again: a $1/$2 winner at $10 an hour. Say they are up two buy-ins three hours into a six hour session.

::: stat $30
What leaving three hours early costs the example player, on average. It is the same $30 whether they are up two buy-ins or down two. The number on the table does not change what the next three hours are worth.
:::

With a stop loss, that $30 buys something: protection against the worse player I become when I am stuck. With a stop win, it is much less clear what it buys. Being up does not usually wreck my game the way being down does.

## What it actually buys

The honest answer is that a stop win buys a feeling. Going home with the win feels better than going home with half of it, and that feeling is worth something to a lot of people.

It is also expensive in a way that is easy to miss. Winning sessions are not that common. For the example player, finishing a six hour session up two buy-ins or more happens about one time in 12. Those sessions are where a winning player's profit comes from. A rule that cuts them short trims the best nights and leaves the worst ones untouched.

If your bankroll is thin enough that giving back a good night would genuinely hurt, that is a real reason to lock it in. But it is a bankroll problem, and the [bankroll guide](/bankroll) is the better fix.

## The version I do use

There is one stop win I can defend, and it is not a fixed number. It is a trailing stop.

Once I am up two buy-ins, I note the high-water mark. From there, if I give back half of what I was up, I leave. Up $400 and it drops to $200, I go home with $200. If it keeps climbing, I keep playing, and the line moves up with it.

That is not about locking in money. It is about the state I wrote about in [the law of averages post](/blog/i-fought-the-law): after a run of everything working, the marginal call starts to feel right and the thin bet starts to feel thick. Giving back half a good night is the clearest signal I have that something has drifted, and it arrives while I still have most of the win to protect.

It also lets a good game stay a good game. If I am up and still playing well, the trailing stop never fires.

## The better reason to leave a winning session

The number is the weakest reason to quit while you are ahead. The best one has nothing to do with my stack.

When I am winning, it is often because someone at the table is losing a lot. When that player leaves, the game I was beating is gone, and it has been replaced by a tougher one. Nobody announces that. The chips in front of me look exactly the same.

So the question I ask when I am up is not *how much am I winning*. It is *is this still the game I sat down in?* If the answer is no, I leave, whatever the number says.

## Write the peak down

None of this works if I am guessing. *I was up about $400 at one point* is a memory, and memories round in whichever direction feels better.

[Log is free, all of it, and works offline with no account](/app). Note where the session peaked and where it finished, and in a few months you will be able to see how often a good night got given back, and whether a trailing stop would have saved any of it.
