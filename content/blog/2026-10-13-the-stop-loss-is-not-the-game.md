After I wrote about [setting a stop loss](/blog/the-loss-is-complete), I noticed something I had not expected. The rule worked. I left when I hit the number. But some nights it changed how I played long before I got anywhere near it.

I like playing poker. That is most of why I am at the table. And a stop loss, if you hold it the wrong way, turns every losing hand into a step toward the moment the fun ends. You stop playing to win and start playing not to get sent home.

That is a real cost, and it is worth fixing, because a stop loss that makes you play scared is costing you money from the other direction.

## What scared play looks like

It does not feel like fear. It feels like being careful.

Down a buy-in and a half, I fold a spot I would normally call, because a loss there would put me close to the line. I check a hand I would bet, because a raise would mean a decision I do not want to face. Each of those folds feels like it is protecting my night. Each of them gives away a little of my edge.

The cards still do not know what I am down. A good call is a good call at the top of the night and two hours into a losing one. The moment the stop loss starts deciding hands, it has stopped being a safety rule and become a second opponent.

## The stop is a seatbelt, not a countdown

The fix that helped me most was understanding how rarely the stop actually comes into play.

::: stat 14 of 15
Six hour sessions in which the example player never once touches a two buy-in stop loss. With a three buy-in stop it is about 129 out of 130.
:::

That is the example player from the [running bad guide](/running-bad), a $1/$2 winner at $10 an hour with ordinary variance. Fourteen nights out of fifteen, the stop loss never matters. It is not a budget I am spending down hand by hand. It is a seatbelt: it does its job on the rare night something goes badly wrong, and the rest of the time I do not think about it.

A seatbelt does not make you drive slower. If it does, something is off with how you are thinking about it.

## Only look at it when you reach for your wallet

The practical change is simple. I do not track how close I am to the stop while I play. I check it in exactly one place: when I am about to buy back in.

That is the moment the stop loss was built for. In between, I am not down a buy-in and a half. I am just playing the hand in front of me with the chips in front of me. The number lives in my pocket, not in my head.

If I catch myself doing the arithmetic in the middle of a hand, that is the signal to take a walk. Not because I am near the stop, but because I have started playing the stop instead of the cards.

## Make it big enough that you can forget it

A stop loss that is too tight is the one that breeds fear. If one ordinary losing stretch can end your night, you will feel it breathing down your neck from the first hand.

That is why I count in buy-ins and set it at two or three, not at some round dollar number that a single cooler can wipe out. And if the number that fits my bankroll feels uncomfortably close, the honest fix is usually not a looser stop. It is a smaller game.

That is also the answer for anyone who mostly wants more time at the table. The same money buys twice as many buy-ins at half the stakes, so the stop moves further away and the night gets longer. If playing is the point, lower stakes are more poker, not less.

## Decide what happens to the night before you sit down

A lot of the fear is really about the evening. If hitting the stop means the night is over, every losing hand is a threat to my plans.

So I make sure it does not mean that. Before I go, I know what I will do if the stop hits: get dinner with whoever came with me, watch the game for a while without a seat, head home and watch something. The poker might end early. The night does not.

It also helps to treat the buy-ins I bring as the price of the evening, decided in advance, the same way I would think about tickets to a game or a night out. Spending it is not a disaster. It is what I already agreed it might cost, and the rest of the time I get the poker for less.

## The rule is there so you can relax

The whole point of deciding the stop ahead of time was to take a decision off my plate while I play. A stop loss that I worry about all night has given me that decision back, plus an extra one.

Set it once. Make it big enough to forget. Check it only when you reach for your wallet. Then go back to the reason you sat down.
