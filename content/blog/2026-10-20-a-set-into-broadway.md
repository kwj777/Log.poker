I looked down at two queens. One player had ace king. The flop came queen, jack, ten.

I had top set. He had the nut straight. He bet $200, I was last to act, and I called. The board never paired, and his straight held.

If you play, you already know there was no folding this one for either of us. If you do not, this is what a cooler looks like: two very good hands, one slightly better, and the chips going the wrong way.

::: figure set-into-broadway
I have the ranks but not the suits, so those are dashed. I do not remember the turn and river, only that neither one paired the board.
:::

Afterward I wanted to know how rare it was. So I asked, and then I checked the answer card by card.

## The flop, once the hands are dealt

Once my queens and his ace king are out, there are 48 cards left. Three of them make a flop, and there are 17,296 ways to pick three cards from 48.

For the flop to be exactly queen, jack, ten, it needs one of the 2 queens I am not holding, one of the 4 jacks and one of the 4 tens. That is 2 × 4 × 4 = 32 flops.

32 out of 17,296 is about 0.19 percent, or roughly 1 in 540.

## The whole thing

That is only the last step. For the hand to happen at all, three things have to line up.

1. **I get two queens.** There are 6 ways to be dealt a pair of queens out of 1,326 starting hands, so 1 in 221.
2. **He gets ace king.** With my queens gone, 50 cards are left. He has 16 ways to hold ace king out of 1,225 possible hands, so about 1 in 77.
3. **The flop comes queen, jack, ten.** The 1 in 540 from above.

Multiply them together and it comes to about 1 in 9.1 million against one particular player.

::: stat 1 in 9 million
The odds of being dealt two queens, a particular opponent holding ace king, and the flop coming queen, jack, ten.
:::

At a full nine handed table, any of my eight opponents could be the one with ace king, which makes it about eight times more likely. Counted properly, it is close to 1 in 1.2 million.

## Every hand is one in a million

Here is the thing I keep coming back to. Nine million sounds like a lightning strike, but almost any hand is that rare if you describe it precisely enough. Two specific hands and three specific ranks on the flop are always going to multiply out to a huge number.

What is not rare is the shape of it. Flopping a set into a straight, a flush into a bigger flush, a full house into a better full house: some version of that happens to everyone who plays enough. I wrote about [three of them in one session](/blog/three-coolers-and-a-fold) just last week. The exact cards were a long shot. Getting coolered was not.

That matters, because "one in nine million" is the kind of number that makes a hand feel like it was aimed at you. It was not. It was a common kind of bad luck wearing a rare set of clothes.

## I was not drawing dead

The number that actually mattered at the table was much smaller and much friendlier.

A straight beats a set, but a full house beats a straight. If the board pairs, I make a full house and win. That can happen a few ways:

- **A jack or a ten on the turn or river.** There are 3 of each left.
- **The last queen.** That gives me four of a kind.
- **The turn and river pairing each other.** Any matching pair on the last two cards, like two sevens, gives me queens full.

Counting every possible turn and river from the flop, with no flush draws on the board, there are 990 of them. I win 341, tie 9 (an ace and a king together put Broadway on the board for both of us) and lose 640.

That is about 34 percent. Roughly one time in three, the hand that looked like a disaster on the flop turns into the one I win.

## What I am taking from it

A cooler does not need a lesson, and I do not think this one has one about how I played it. Nobody is folding a set or the nuts on that flop, and nobody should. I called the $200, I was a little better than one in three to win, and I lost. That is a cooler, not a mistake.

What it does have is a reminder about which number to listen to. The 1 in 9 million is a good story. The 34 percent is the truth about the hand. One of them makes you feel unlucky. The other tells you that you were still very much in it, and that losing it, the way I did, is the ordinary result two times out of three.
